Over the years, I have worked in many different industries and for companies that came in all different shapes and sizes and I've come to a realisation.

If I were designing an accelerated 10-year curriculum for a well-rounded engineer, I'd mandate two stops: a startup, then a consultancy.

Is it the only path? No.

Is it the quickest way to get trial-by-fired into a battle-tested engineer? Absolutely.

## Why Join a Start-up?

Not all startups are the same, but they often share a scrappy nature. They are an environment where a small number of people with a big
dream are trying to get a monumental amount of work done. You may have been hired as a software engineer, but you will end up _doing
customer research, doing site visits, analytics monitoring, selling the product at conventions, setting up
a <dfn><abbr title="Continuous Integration">CI</abbr></dfn> server, candidate
interviews, <dfn><abbr title="Quality Assurance">QA</abbr></dfn>, building a marketing website, organising office events, architecting new
features, resolving interpersonal disputes within the team, travelling, automating internal workflows and much much more_. It is true what
they say, in a start-up you wear many different hats.

You learn that you don't need to be an expert to wear the hat; you just need to be willing to figure it out. This is a great mindset to have
as it unlocks future potential you didn't even know you had.

You may also end up knowing your biggest, or most quirky customers personally through many hours of face-to-face customer interviews,
feedback sessions or them coming to visit you at a convention. You develop a deep empathy for the user as you know their job inside out so
you can build the best possible product.

You may develop an unhealthy attachment to the codebase, your baby you've poured blood sweat and tears into over the years, and then see it
sail off into the distance when a rewrite renders it irrelevant. This is a lesson worth learning once and keeping with you for the rest of
your career. How to care for the code and codebase without being emotionally attached to it. It's not yours, it's the company's.

You get feedback, sometimes good feedback, sometimes very directly bad feedback when that change you thought would improve things just made
someone's workflow 10x more complicated. You sometimes get no feedback or fanfare on a big feature you worked really hard on, which in
itself is a form of feedback. You build resilience, all feedback is good to know, it's not a reflection on your self-worth, it's an
opportunity to course correct but also to reflect, what did we miss in our planning? What didn't we know before we made the bet on this
feature?

Feedback also teaches you that not every customer reacts to unpolished software the same way. You learn that different customers are willing
to put up with different levels of polish in the system at different times. To paraphrase [Crossing the Chasm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crossing_the_Chasm) (see image below), Innovators love the technology
itself and don't mind if it's held together by duct tape. Early Adopters buy into the transformative vision, so they willingly tolerate some
instability and shifting UIs if it gives them a competitive leap. By the time you get to the early and late majority, they're expecting a
consistent user experience with a good level of stability. You may never meet the laggards.

![Crossing the Chasm chart](/blog-images/crossing-the-chasm.webp)

So not all users are equal, not because of how big their contract is but because of where they sit on the adoption curve and what they're
willing to tolerate while you build the future.

You will work at a fast pace, really fast. You are building a product from the ground up. You're trying different things, you're trying to
find <dfn><abbr title="Product Market Fit">PMF</abbr></dfn> which means sometimes an idea needs to materialise in the app ASAP so it can be
demo'd to a customer in a few days. You learn to manage when to cut corners and which corners to cut to make that happen, and how to treat
technical debt as an active trade-off rather than just another ticket ("Feature X: Tech Debt") buried in the backlog that never gets
prioritised.

## Why Join a Consultancy?

Consultancy work takes many forms, such as team-augmentation, time-and-materials work,
fixed-cost <dfn><abbr title="Statement Of Work">SOW</abbr></dfn>s, greenfield work, brownfield work, integrations work etc. Each of these
brings with it new challenges and new experiences.

Over the years in a consultancy, you get used to putting a team together, figuring out how you all gel and just as soon as you're all
humming along, the project is over and the team disbanded. This used to frustrate me to no end, you spend an inordinate amount of time in
the _forming_ and _storming_ phase, before you get to _norming_ and _performing_
[(Tuckman's stages of group development)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tuckman%27s_stages_of_group_development) for the length of engagement
on smaller projects. Do it enough times though and you get really good experience in understanding people, personalities and teams. You have
lots of experimentation opportunities as you put into practice something you learned during your last team forming experience.

You learn to get into an old unloved codebase, figure out how to surgically insert the functionality you were required to do without
disturbing existing functionality and triggering a 6 month <dfn><abbr title="User Acceptance Testing">UAT</abbr></dfn> process. This again
is a good skill to have, mapping out risky parts of the codebase, knowing what relies on what so that pulling on a thread in one file
doesn't accidentally unravel a sheep in a totally unrelated directory.

It's also a skill not to get frustrated by legacy code. Fact is, someone wrote that at some point in the past doing the best work they could
with the time, resources, technology and constraints they had available to them at the point. You are now visiting with better tooling and
probably different constraints, you can't judge the work too harshly. Just acknowledge it as a fact and figure out how to work with it. You
might get an opportunity to modernise it, if you do, someone else may come along later judging _your_ work.

You learn to have your personal preferences but set them aside when it's necessary. You may have joined an existing team, or augmenting a
client team. They have standards and opinions already set in place. You can't come in and do everything your way, that'd create 2 different
ways of doing things in one codebase. You learn to adopt practices where you need to, even if it's something you wouldn't personally
gravitate towards. You can always influence decisions later when you've built up rapport and trust in the team.

You become comfortable in unfamiliar domains. Client A might be a media project, Client B might be working in fire tech in a government
agency, while doing a sales call for an Agentic AI project with Client C. You learn to apply your expertise where you can, regardless of the
domain. You develop processes for fast-tracking deep immersion in a new domain. I like to have NotebookLM generate audio for me to get the
broad brush strokes before diving into the details.

You learn to work with a range of different customers in a range of different environments and organisations. You learn to map out
stakeholders, org structures and more importantly shadow org structures, where the power and influence _actually_ lies. Who do we need to
keep happy, who needs to be consulted vs informed? There's often politics at play, sometimes a decision isn't made until it's been written
in an email and is traceable. Sometimes a decision is made, but then overruled because the person who made the decision wasn't truly
empowered to make that decision. You learn to spot these nuances after being burned a few times.

You will probably do an agile-ish process, we all typically do. Except for a fixed-cost SOW project, that agile process might uncover lots
of important stuff that isn't in the SOW. You will at some point deliver a client a product worth 200% of the price they actually paid for
because you always want to deliver "the product they need, not the product they thought they wanted", but this will burn out your team. You
only estimated the team size and time required on the size of the SOW. You will develop processes to deal with this, padding on the
estimates, trying to explode SOWs out to the actual amount of work you might end up doing before estimating. Asking lots of questions up
front to uncover the areas of ambiguity where 30 features are likely to live.

You hone your process through repetition, projects come and go, teams spin up and spin down, pre-sales call after pre-sales call,
presentations, presentations, presentations. Whatever you're doing, you're doing it many times, if you improve just a bit each time, over
years, this is significant. You build confidence and style with each iteration too.

## So Why Do The Start-up First, Then a Consultancy?

Start-ups teach you how to figure things out on the fly and instill the belief that you can solve whatever is thrown at you. A consultancy
then gives you the playground to refine that raw skill through disciplined repetition, varied architectures, and structured client
environments. 

Emerging from this specific pairing turns you into an incredibly versatile, battle-tested engineer. You become employable in virtually any
environment because you aren't bound by a single domain, stack, or way of working.

## Timeframes

In my case, this was roughly 10 years of experience split across both environments. Natural career progression typically happens over a
decade regardless, but experiencing both sides of this coin has definitely accelerated my trajectory. It made me a significantly stronger, more pragmatic,
and more confident engineer than I ever would have been staying in a single traditional environment.

## What about Big Tech? What about doing it backwards?

Sure, starting in a consultancy teaches you discipline and testing first, so you don’t build a startup house of cards.
And Big Tech teaches you scale you'll never see in an agency. But starting in the startup fire forces you to cultivate sheer resourcefulness before you ever learn
to polish it. That sequence builds an engineer who simply doesn't panic in a sticky situation.

## Soft Skills Are Just as Important as Hard Skills

At the end of the day, software engineering is as much about navigating ambiguity, human dynamics, and trade-offs as it is about writing
clean code. Startups teach you how to build from scratch at breakneck speed; consultancies teach you how to adapt, influence, and execute
repeatedly across complex organisational boundaries and domains.

A huge portion of high-impact engineering happens away from the keyboard. By intentionally putting yourself in environments that force you
to communicate, adapt, and build empathy early on, you fast-track the exact soft skills that will set you up to excel wherever your career
takes you next.

| SOW                                       | Statement Of Work                                              |
| PMF                                       | Product Market Fit                                             |
